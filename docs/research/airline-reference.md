# Airline Reference — Callsigns & Brand Colors

Purpose: quick-lookup table for identifying airlines from ADS-B callsigns (ICAO 3-letter code + telephony prefix as broadcast on flight idents) and their brand colors. "IATA" is the 2-character abbreviation (used on tickets/boarding passes, NOT what appears in ADS-B). "ICAO Code" is what appears in the ADS-B/Mode-S ident. Colors are approximate hex values based on public livery/brand identity, not official brand-guide values.

## Military & Government Identifiers (ADS-B)

Military aircraft don't use an IATA/ICAO airline-code system. Instead they're identified two ways in ADS-B: (1) an **ICAO 24-bit hex address** from a block set aside for government/military use, and (2) a **spoken tactical callsign** (word + mission number) broadcast in the flight-ID field, functioning like the airline "Callsign Word" above but usually mission-specific rather than fixed to one squadron.

### Hex address block (US)
- The entire United States is allocated the ICAO 24-bit hex range **A00000–AFFFFF**. Civil aircraft (N-number registrations) are mathematically derived from within this block.
- US military aircraft are commonly observed clustering in the **AE0000–AFFFFF** sub-range. This is a practical/informal pattern used by tracking tools (not an officially published subdivision), so treat it as a strong heuristic, not a guarantee.
- Aircraft in the **F00000+** range (outside any country's normal block) typically indicate flight-test, anonymized/privacy addressing, or other special-use assignments — not necessarily military.
- Some ISR aircraft (e.g., RC-135 Rivet Joint) have been documented broadcasting a **different country's** hex block temporarily to obscure identity; this is a known but rare anomaly, not the norm.

### Important caveats for LLM use

1. Unlike airline callsigns, most **fighter, bomber, and tactical** callsign words rotate by squadron, exercise, or even by sortie — treat any specific fighter/tactical callsign as unreliable for long-term identification without a corresponding hex-code lookup.
2. **REACH and SAM are the two most reliable, persistent, non-rotating callsign words** to pattern-match in general civil ADS-B feeds, since they denote standing mission types rather than a single squadron.
3. Many military aircraft use **ADS-B Out with reduced/blocked data** (no position or altitude) for operational security; presence of a hex code with no other data is common and expected, not necessarily anomalous.
4. Hex-code-to-tail-number mapping for military aircraft is not authoritatively published by the FAA/DoD the way civil N-numbers are — third-party crowd-sourced databases (e.g., tar1090-db) are the best available reference and may contain gaps or errors.

## Usage Notes for LLM Consumption

1. **IATA** (2 characters, e.g. `DL`) is the code used on tickets, boarding passes, and airport signage — it is *not* what appears in an ADS-B ident.
2. **ICAO Code** (3 letters, e.g. `DAL`) begins an ADS-B/Mode-S flight ident (e.g., `DAL1234` = Delta flight 1234, spoken as "Delta 1234").
3. **ATC Radio Callsign** = the spoken/telephony word ATC and pilots use for that ICAO prefix — useful for matching audio transcripts or ATC chatter to a carrier.
4. Regional carriers often fly in the paint/livery of their mainline partner but broadcast their **own** ICAO prefix and often their own IATA code too — use the regional's ICAO prefix for accurate ADS-B identification, and cross-reference "Operates As" when the ident and livery seem to disagree.
5. Some carriers post-merger briefly share codes (e.g., Alaska/Hawaiian); verify against registration/fleet data during active integration periods.
6. Colors are for visual/branding association only (e.g., generating livery descriptions, logos, or UI theming) — treat as approximate; official brand guidelines may differ slightly.
